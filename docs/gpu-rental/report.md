# Rent Vast Blackwells hourly, keep state elsewhere

The cheapest SSH-capable way to rent the machine V2D Track 3 needs is an **RTX PRO 6000 96 GB on the Vast.ai marketplace**. It costs about **$1.04–1.18/h on demand and $0.53–0.59/h interruptible**, including a right-sized 200 GB disk. Billing is per second after a $5 deposit, and hosts with driver 580 or newer can be selected by filter ([Vast offers API](https://console.vast.ai/api/v0/bundles/), [Vast quickstart](https://docs.vast.ai/guides/get-started/quickstart)). That undercuts every fixed-price option meeting our spec, from RunPod Community at $1.69 and Nebius at $1.80 to RunPod Secure at $2.49. It also beats switching to cheaper 48 GB cards or to datacenter HBM cards, because the RTX PRO 6000 is also the cheapest card per trained policy. Every relevant provider is pay-as-you-go with no commitment, so the levers that matter are operational. Destroy instances instead of stopping them. Keep about 100 GB of rebuildable bootstrap archives in Cloudflare R2, which costs about $1 for the whole window, and keep the few GB that matter in the free tiers of Backblaze B2 and Hugging Face. Run batch training on interruptible hosts. On that plan, the first end-to-end public-episode test costs **about $4–12**, the first 20-policy leaderboard submission **about $35–50**, and the full 100–150 GPU-hour campaign to 2026-11-04 **about $80–155, with a central estimate near $110**. The same campaign costs $170–255 on RunPod Community and $255–385 on RunPod Secure with a network volume. Colab Pro is cheaper per hour than Vast on-demand, but it cannot be driven over SSH and does not keep state, so it should only absorb overflow work that a person runs. A GCP $300 trial credit could make the campaign nearly free if Spot G4 quota arrives, but that is a lottery ticket, not a plan. Three unknowns set the error bars: marketplace prices are a 2026-10-18 snapshot, interruptible preemption rates are unmeasured, and FlashCHORD's ability to resume from checkpoints is unverified.

## The RTX PRO 6000 is the cheapest card per policy, not only the fastest

The first question is which GPU to rent, because a card that is cheaper per hour can be more expensive per trained policy. Our own measurement is the anchor. FlashCHORD's Sharpa FlashSAC recipe ran at **~45,300 env steps/s with a 40.5 GB peak** on an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition, with JAX preallocation disabled. A 250M-step policy therefore takes **1.5–2 h** (`HANDOFF.md`, 2026-10-12 Colab smoke test). No public benchmark runs MuJoCo-Warp on an H100, A100 or B200 next to an RTX card. The MuJoCo-Warp developers run their nightly benchmarks on an RTX 6000 Ada ([MJWarp nightly](https://google-deepmind.github.io/mujoco_warp/nightly/)), and their October 2026 pull requests use an "otherwise idle RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell" ([mujoco_warp PR #1748](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_warp/pull/1748)). The closest cross-GPU evidence cuts against HBM datacenter parts. On MuJoCo Playground's contact-rich LEAP-hand task, **eight H100s trained only about 3x faster than one RTX 4090** ([MuJoCo Playground](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08844v1)).

The notes include a spec-based cost model, which is an estimate rather than a measurement. It assumes half the runtime scales with SM count × clock and half with memory bandwidth. At the cheapest October listings, it puts the RTX PRO 6000 at about **$1.1–1.2 per 250M-step policy**. The RTX A6000 ($1.31), L40S ($1.33) and RTX 6000 Ada ($1.49) come out roughly even, but each takes 1.5–2.6x longer per policy ([computeprices RTX PRO 6000](https://computeprices.com/gpus/rtx-pro-6000)). The H100, H200 and B200 come out **2–10x more expensive per policy**, even under assumptions that favor bandwidth. The A100 has only 19.5 TFLOPS of FP32, 0.16x the PRO 6000 ([NVIDIA A100](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/a100/)), which leaves its cost per policy anywhere from $1.4 to $14.

Memory decides the rest. Our 40.5 GB peak is real usage and fills 84% of a 48 GB card. AWS's L40S instance reports only about 45 GiB of usable VRAM ([SpareCores g6e.4xlarge](https://sparecores.com/server/aws/g6e.4xlarge)), and our requirements note says FlashCHORD's full training wants at least 46,000 MiB free (`reports/v2d-challenge/track3-requirements.md`). A 48 GB card therefore saves cents per hour while risking a failed or reconfigured run. That is a poor trade when the PRO 6000 already wins per policy.

The variant inside the PRO 6000 family also matters. The Server Edition's power limit is configurable "up to 600W" ([NVIDIA Server Edition](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/rtx-pro-6000-blackwell-server-edition/)). The Workstation Edition has 1,792 GB/s of memory bandwidth against the Server Edition's 1,597 ([NVIDIA Workstation](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/products/workstations/professional-desktop-gpus/rtx-pro-6000/)). The 300 W Max-Q, which supplies the cheapest marketplace offers, has no published specs, so its throughput is unknown.

Packing two runs per card deserves one test. nvidia-smi's 100% utilization only means some kernel was executing ([nvidia-smi docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/deploy/nvidia-smi/index.html)), so two 40.5 GB runs on one 96 GB card could raise aggregate throughput. However, two of our perception jobs ran out of memory when they shared the Colab G4 (`HANDOFF.md`, 2026-10-13). Packing remains a hypothesis until measured.

Vast's lead is large but conditional. In the public offers API snapshot (15:27 UTC), the cheapest offers meeting our full spec were an on-demand Max-Q in California at **$0.989** (unverified host, 16 vCPU, 123 GB RAM) and a Workstation Edition in Australia at **$1.122** (32 vCPU, 121 GB RAM, driver 595.84). Every WS and Max-Q offer, and 13 of 14 Server Edition offers, already ran driver 580 or newer ([Vast offers API](https://console.vast.ai/api/v0/bundles/)). The catch is that each host sets its own storage and bandwidth prices. Storage ran from $0.02 to $1.00 per GB-month. Download bandwidth had a median of $2.67/TB but reached $53/TB, and upload reached $82/TB. The cheapest *verified* compliant host charged $0.533/GB-month for storage and $53/$82 per TB for bandwidth. Offers should therefore be filtered on all three prices, not just GPU $/h. For hardware, the notes propose filtering on `cuda_vers>=13.0 cpu_cores_effective>=16 cpu_ram>=65536 reliability>=0.98`, avoiding the `driver_version` field because it compares as a string ([Vast SDK docs](https://docs.vast.ai/sdk/python)). Use the console or API, not the marketing site: Vast's own RTX PRO 6000 page said "No current offers" while the API listed 29 ([Vast GPU page](https://vast.ai/pricing/gpu/RTX-PRO-6000-WS)).

Fixed-price clouds are moving the wrong way. RunPod raised Secure Cloud prices several times in 2026 ([pricingsaas](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/runpod/diffs/2026W42)). Jarvislabs raised the RTX PRO 6000 from $1.89 to $2.19 on October 5, citing a "global shortage of GPU memory" ([Jarvislabs](https://jarvislabs.ai/updates/gpu-pricing-october-2026)). Prices are unlikely to fall before November 4.

Every option in the table is pay-as-you-go with no commitment, and commitments would buy almost nothing in a 17-day window. Jarvislabs offers 2–9% off ([Jarvislabs](https://jarvislabs.ai/pricing)), Verda 2–8% on terms under two years ([Verda](https://verda.com/pricing)), and Vast's discount of up to 50% requires 1–6 month reservations ([Vast pricing](https://vast.ai/pricing)).

## Disposable GPUs and a $1 object store beat persistent volumes

Storage is cheap; storage mistakes are not. The first saving is right-sizing. The itemized need is about **150 GB, not 500**: 70 GB of depth caches, 15 GB of environments, 10 GB of weights, 2 GB of data and 17–51 GB of checkpoints. Training-only hosts need a fraction of that because they never touch the depth caches. Only the perception and debugging box needs about 200 GB.

The second saving is never treating a stopped instance as storage, because "stopped" rarely means free. Vast bills storage "continuously while your instance exists, regardless of running state," usually at a higher rate when stopped, and destroying the instance is the only way to stop the charge ([Vast pricing](https://docs.vast.ai/documentation/instances/pricing), [Vast storage](https://docs.vast.ai/documentation/instances/storage/types)). A stopped Vast instance's restart also "may wait indefinitely for GPU availability" ([Vast manage instances](https://docs.vast.ai/guides/instances/manage-instances)). RunPod charges $0.20/GB-month for a stopped volume disk, and the pod can come back with **zero GPUs** ([RunPod zero-GPU doc](https://docs.runpod.io/pods/troubleshooting/zero-gpus)). Verda keeps billing shut-down instances ([Verda](https://docs.verda.com/cpu-and-gpu-instances/shutdown-hibernate-and-delete/)), Hyperstack keeps billing VMs in SHUTOFF, and Lambda cannot pause at all.

The durable design keeps state in three tiers, none of them on a GPU host:

| Tier | Contents | Where | Cost | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Code | The repository | Git | Free; submissions need a commit URL in a repository shared with the organizers anyway | — |
| Rebuildable bulk, ~100 GB | Environment archives, weights, LFS assets and depth caches | **Cloudflare R2** | $0.015/GB-month with 10 GB free and **no egress fees**, so **about $0.76 for the whole window** however often we restore | [R2 pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/) |
| Durable small set | Policies (4.9 MB each), 161 MB training-state checkpoints, references, scores and logs | **Backblaze B2**, **Hugging Face** and the Mac | B2: first 10 GB free; Hugging Face: 100 GB of private storage free | [B2 pricing](https://www.backblaze.com/cloud-storage/pricing), [HF storage limits](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/storage-limits) |

Hugging Face storage buckets are designed for training checkpoints and rolling backups ([HF Storage Buckets](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/storage-buckets)). Wasabi is a poor fit because of its 1 TB monthly minimum and 90-day minimum retention ([Wasabi FAQ](https://wasabi.com/pricing/faq)).

A RunPod network volume would cost about **$7.80 for 200 GB** over the window, or $19.60 for 500 GB. However, it works only with Secure Cloud pods and is locked to one datacenter ([RunPod network volumes](https://docs.runpod.io/storage/network-volumes)). That makes it worth buying only if we commit to RunPod Secure, which costs about $0.80/h more than Vast.

Using the GPU only when needed then becomes simple arithmetic. Rebuilding the full stack took about 10 minutes on Colab (`HANDOFF.md`). At $1.18/h, a bootstrap therefore costs about **$0.20** plus host bandwidth: about $0.08 for 30 GB at the median rate, or $1.60 on a $53/TB host. An instance left idle overnight burns about **$14**, more than the entire first end-to-end test. So destroy the instance at the end of every work block, and before any expected gap longer than about 30 minutes, after pushing results to the tiers above.

To keep bootstraps fast, put the uv cache on the same filesystem as the venv, which uv says matters for performance ([uv cache docs](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/concepts/cache/)), and run `uv sync --frozen`. A tarball of the built venv stored in R2 could cut bootstrap time further; that is a hypothesis to measure.

We also propose a small controller on the Mac. It would poll instance state through the provider API and over SSH, and destroy finished, idle or preempted instances. Without it, an outbid interruptible instance sits paused while still billing storage.

Funding Vast in small top-ups turns the prepaid balance into a spending cap. This is safe only because nothing durable lives on instances. Vast's billing page says that without a saved card, instances and their data are destroyed after a grace period at zero balance, though Vast's documentation is inconsistent on this ([Vast billing](https://docs.vast.ai/documentation/reference/billing)).

Credentials need the same care. Vast advises using its Cloud Sync feature only on datacenters marked Secure ([Vast manage instances](https://docs.vast.ai/guides/instances/manage-instances)). On cheap unverified hosts, either inject bucket-scoped, write-only keys as environment variables or pull results back to the Mac over SSH. Rotate all keys after November 4.

## The cheapest plan costs about $12, $40 and $110

The estimates assume **2.0 h per 250M-step policy**, the top of the measured range, which covers JIT compilation and overhead. On-demand time is priced at **$1.18/h**, the cheapest compliant Vast WS host with a 200 GB disk, or $1.58–1.75/h on a verified host. Interruptible time is priced at **$0.55–0.80/h**, spanning the bottom of the price ladder to its median, because 5–10 simultaneous hosts cannot all land on the cheapest offer. Interruptible training carries a 25% rework allowance for preemptions. Everything has to finish before the leaderboard freezes on **November 4, 2026 at 5:00 p.m. EST** ([V2D Challenge](https://nvidia-isaac.github.io/video_to_data/v2d_challenge/)). Alternatives in the table are priced at a single flat rate with no rework allowance, even for spot, which flatters GCP Spot.

**(a) First end-to-end test: about $12.** The test runs one public episode through perception, hands, reference building, a 20M-step smoke run, a full 250M-step policy, the official evaluator and local scoring. It takes about 3 GPU-hours if nothing breaks, and 8–10 hours for a realistic SSH-driven debugging day. Use one Vast on-demand host, either Workstation or Server Edition, with reliability of at least 0.98, low storage and bandwidth prices, and about 200 GB of disk. Avoid Max-Q until it is benchmarked. Do not use an interruptible host here, because losing the machine wastes human time. The day costs about **$12**, or $5–17 on a verified host. The same instance should build the R2 bootstrap archive once.

**(b) First 20-policy submission: about $40.** About six on-demand hours cover perception and references for the 20 evaluation episodes, evaluation, scoring and the controller box. The 40 hours of training run on interruptible hosts, for roughly **$35–50** in total, against about $54 if everything ran on demand.

How many hosts run at once is itself a cost lever. The cheapest compliant interruptible PRO 6000s were $0.43–0.53/h, but the medians were $0.73–1.03. Renting 20 hosts at once therefore means climbing the price ladder and paying for 20 bootstraps. Running **5–10 hosts with 2–4 sequential policies each** keeps the batch near the bottom of the ladder, at a cost of about 4–8 h of wall-clock time. Four-GPU hosts at $1.00–1.32 per GPU would amortize one bootstrap over four runs. However, the cheapest 4-GPU boxes in the snapshot offered only 12–24 vCPU and 53–63 GB RAM per GPU, just under our spec ([Vast offers API](https://console.vast.ai/api/v0/bundles/)).

At the bottom of the ladder ($0.55), interruptible beats on-demand ($1.18) unless preemptions more than double the GPU-hours per completed policy. At the median ($0.80), the margin shrinks to about 1.5x. Outbid Vast instances are stopped and "may wait long to resume" ([Vast rental types](https://docs.vast.ai/rental-types)), so the controller should destroy and relaunch them rather than wait.

**(c) Full campaign: about $110.** The campaign is 100–150 GPU-hours in total. About 30–40 of those hours are on demand: debugging, perception, evaluation and final-days runs. The other 60–110 hours are interruptible training across two or three 20-policy cycles. Adding about $5 for R2 storage and Vast bandwidth gives **$80–155, with a central estimate near $110**. In the final three days, when submissions are unlimited (`reports/v2d-challenge/track3-requirements.md`), any run that must finish before the freeze should move to verified on-demand hosts. A premium of about $0.60–1.20/h is trivial against missing the deadline. Fund the account in increments, and set a working budget of $150 with a hard ceiling of $200 to absorb price moves.

Three free options are worth pursuing in parallel, though none belongs on the critical path. The most valuable is the **GCP trial credit**: upgrading the trial to a paid account keeps the remaining $300 usable for the original 90 days, enough for about 184 h of Spot G4 at $1.63 ([GCP Free Trial](https://docs.cloud.google.com/free/docs/free-cloud-features)). It pays off only if the account has never been a paying Google Cloud customer, if quota is granted for both `PREEMPTIBLE_NVIDIA_RTX_PRO_6000_GPUS` and the global "GPUs (all regions)" ([GCP quotas](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/resource-usage)), and if the credit covers Spot GPUs after the upgrade, which is unverified. Quota is the weakest link: community reports range from minutes to a week, and one paid account sat at zero quota for over 50 hours ([Google Dev forum](https://discuss.google.dev/t/cloud-run-gpu-quota-not-activated-after-50-hours-on-paid-account/323795)). The second option costs one email, because the challenge FAQ calls **organizer GPU credits** "under review" (`reports/v2d-challenge/track3-requirements.md`). The third is the **Colab units** we have already paid for; they are sunk cost and should be spent first, on runs a person drives.

## Four day-one measurements collapse the error bars

Most of the remaining uncertainty can be removed on the first rented host for under $5. The first measurement is a **benchmark of the actual host**: run the exact recipe for 10–20M steps after JIT warm-up and record steps/s, peak memory and `nvidia-smi -q -d POWER,CLOCK`. That replaces modeled cost per policy with measured cost, at under $1 per GPU type by the notes' estimate, and shows whether Max-Q hosts are bargains or traps. The second is a **packing test** with two runs on one 96 GB card. If aggregate steps/s rises well beyond a single run, the 20-policy batch needs half as many hosts; if the runs go out of memory, as our perception jobs did, the question is closed.

The third, and most consequential, is a **kill-and-resume test**: kill a run at about 20M steps and resume it from the 161 MB training-state checkpoint. If resume works, a preemption costs minutes and interruptible hosts can carry nearly all training. If it fails, every preemption costs a full restart, and late-campaign runs should move to on-demand. The fourth is to **time the bootstrap and log preemptions**: time the R2-to-host restore with `rclone copy --transfers 16`, and log every preemption on interruptible hosts. Both numbers feed directly into the idle and rework rules above.

## Conclusion

At about $110 for the whole campaign, GPU rent stops being the binding constraint. Wall-clock time before November 4, and having machines an agent can drive reliably, now are. The money is best spent buying back time where it matters most: on-demand hosts in the final three days, and verified hosts if unverified ones prove flaky on the interactive day. The biggest savings come from operating discipline, not from finding a cheaper provider. That discipline is destroy-not-stop, right-sized disks, 5–10 hosts wide, and interruptible hosts paired with a verified resume path. In Vast's own snapshot, the gap between the cheapest and the median interruptible host ($0.30–0.50/h) was as large as the gap between Vast on-demand and Colab.

Because every piece of state lives in git, R2, B2 and Hugging Face rather than on a GPU, the plan is portable. Moving to RunPod Community, to GCP credit or to a teammate's Colab session costs one roughly 10-minute bootstrap. That portability is the real hedge against 2026's rising GPU prices and against marketplace supply vanishing before the freeze.
