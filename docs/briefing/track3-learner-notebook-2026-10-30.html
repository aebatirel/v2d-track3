Before you start

Words this notebook uses

reference
The motion the robot should reproduce, built per episode from the video. It holds two robot-hand trajectories retargeted from the person's hands, plus each object's path from our perception, plus contact labels.
residual action
The policy outputs a correction on top of the reference's hand targets. Zero action means "follow the reference exactly" through the robot's PD servos.
zero-action replay
Running the reference with zero action through the evaluator's physics. It shows whether the plan alone moves the objects correctly, before any training. Our cheapest test.
sim_vs_ref
The kit's ADD-AUC of a simulated rollout against our reference. It is our only score on eval episodes. Across 27 public policies it correlates 0.997 with the true score M, but it cannot see errors in the reference itself.
floor
sim_vs_ref of leaving every object where it starts. A policy below its floor is doing harm.
draw
One official-evaluator rollout of a checkpoint. The evaluator is not reproducible, so draws of one checkpoint can differ by up to 0.7 on some episodes.
twin
A public episode with the same kind of objects as an eval episode. Changes are tested there against mocap before they touch eval episodes.
VOC
Virtual object controller: a training-only force that pushes each object along its reference path. A curriculum fades it to zero. The evaluator never uses it.
seed
The training random seed: 42 by default, 43–46 for second tries.
carve
A change to an object's simulator collision shape only, such as cutting a board's slot or emptying a cup's cavity. The submitted mesh is unchanged.
pre-registered
The rule for choosing between candidates is written into the work log before any result exists. Every eval batch since 24 Oct has one.
Part 1

What a learner trains in

Every policy is trained in NVIDIA FlashCHORD, at the evaluator's pinned commit 8c778f9. We never modify the evaluator. A checkpoint stores its fully resolved configuration, and the official evaluator rebuilds the simulation from it. So an embodiment or reward change we train with travels inside the checkpoint, and the evaluator honours it.

Physics and robot

  • The policy runs at 20 Hz. Physics runs at 100 Hz (5 substeps) on MuJoCo via Newton, with a pyramidal friction cone, friction 1.0 and a 512-constraint cap. The cap overflows on cluttered scenes.
  • Two floating Sharpa Wave hands with 22 finger joints each. The wrist is a soft spring: kp 25 N/m, kd 6, 45 N maximum. The ball joint for rotation has 25 N·m/rad. Fingers have kp 1.7.
  • The hands' own weight is compensated; a held object's is not. Every object is a 0.3 kg placeholder, so carrying one sags the soft wrist 11.8 cm, more than half of its 20 cm action range M.
  • Hands pass through the table (robot–support collision is off), so fingers can get under a flat object.

Policy and learner

  • Action: 56 numbers, 2 hands × (3 wrist position + 3 wrist rotation + 22 fingers), as a residual on the reference. The actor's ±1 stretches to at most ±0.2 m and ±1.0 rad. The gain near zero is 0.05 m and 0.15 rad. Actions are smoothed with an EMA of 0.3.
  • Observation (about 504 numbers for one object): wrist pose and velocity, finger angles, object poses, the next frame's command deltas, recent actions, and each link's contact point and force direction. It has no look-ahead beyond one frame and no object velocity; the critic additionally sees both.
  • Learner: FlashSAC, an off-policy actor-critic with two distributional critics (101 atoms). Hidden sizes are 256 (actor) and 512 (critic). Batch 2,048, n-step 3, γ 0.99, learning rate 3e-4 → 1.5e-4, replay 4.19M. Checkpoints every 10,000,384 steps.

The curriculum

Four properties of this machine drove most of our work:

  1. The soft wrist cannot both carry a 0.3 kg object and follow fast human motion. At real playback speed it saturates on 23 of 27 moving public object tracks E, from the carry-control study.
  2. The object reward measures points 1 m from the centre, so it saturates quickly with rotation error. Past about 0.5 rad it gives almost no gradient (Part 4).
  3. The assist torque uses one scalar inertia per object, which is unstable for long thin objects such as the spoon.
  4. The evaluator's conditions (frame-0 start, no assist, no terminations) appear in training only late, and never in a 70M run.

Part 4

Learner 1: the standard learner

Plain RL from scratch for 250M steps on the human-derived reference, with four changes of ours: a cleaned reference, contact-anchored retargeting, FlashDexRetarget-style rewards and a fixed object assist. It wins where the task is contact-rich and the human's own motion is the best plan: sweeping, slotting boards, lifting a lid, placing into a rack.

4.1 Bring-up: the reference was the first enemy (20 Oct)

  • Floor bug. ep12's first 250M policy scored AUC 0.009 M. The table fit chose the floor (55 % of points vs 39 % for the table), so both objects dropped 55–60 cm at the start. Fixed with a sequential RANSAC and a support check, then retrained.
  • Wrist jumps. The robot wrist jumped up to 30.9 cm per frame while the human palm moved at most 2.6 cm (ep44). The cause is in NVIDIA's retargeting IK, which weights the wrist position 0.2 against 1.0 per fingertip. --wrist_cost 3.0 cut the jumps to 4.6 cm at a cost of 1–2 cm of fingertip error. Bone normalisation and a scale change did not help.
  • First valid score: ep12 0.890 (sim_vs_ref 0.863). An oracle run with mocap object paths scored 0.858, so on this episode the robot sets the score, not perception M.
  • Single-seed results mislead. With wrist cost 3.0, ep12 scored 0.604 (a failure) while ep27 scored 0.965 and ep2 0.926. Rule adopted: never adopt a change on one seed.
  • Rejected: the Viterbi hand-identity linker (fixed one hand-over, added jumps elsewhere) and the kp 400 wrist for plain RL (ep12 0.860 vs 0.890).

  • Engine trap. Newton splits a vertex-coloured mesh into one shape per colour (174 per board), and each goes through convex decomposition × 4,096 worlds, so the ep24 trainings were killed for memory. Fixed with colourless simulator copies.
  • Hands overlapping at reset. On ep24 the two robot hands interpenetrated by 6–14 mm and a finger sat 12.7 mm inside a board. Board 1 popped 15–20° on the first step, so every frame-0 episode terminated immediately. Fixed with --hand_sep 0.04 --lead_in 10: 0.080 → 0.232, still at the floor.
  • Penetration everywhere. The robot hand sat more than 2 mm inside something on 63–88 % of frames (p95 16–20 mm) on easy and hard episodes alike; the human fingertips, 0 %. The evaluation starts without the training's assist, so these overlaps become pops and drops.

4.3 Contact-anchored retargeting (21–22 Oct)

The owner's idea: "snap the fingertips, let the wrist follow". pipeline/contact_retarget.py rebuilds each robot-hand frame so the pads touch where the human touched and nothing penetrates:

  1. Human fingertips within 2.5 cm of an object surface become contact targets, projected onto the surface. Runs shorter than 3 frames are dropped; longer ones are smoothed over 5 frames in the object's frame.
  2. The wrist and 22 finger joints are solved per frame by a hand-written Levenberg–Marquardt. The residual terms are fingertip-to-target distance, penetration (from CPU MuJoCo on the scene's own convex pieces, including the table and the other hand), closeness to the original retarget, and continuity with the previous frame.
  3. The wrist may move at most ±6 cm / ±0.5 rad from the original. Frames without contact relax back toward it. With --rest_settle 1, resting objects are seated on the table in their nearest stable pose.

4.4 FlashDexRetarget-style rewards (23 Oct, 00:50–04:40 TRT)

We ported the reward ideas of FlashDexRetarget (FDR) into FlashCHORD's own term slots. Because the term names stay the same, the unpatched official evaluator still loads the checkpoint (ops/box/fc_v2d_fdr.py, switched on in ops/box/lib.sh):

  • Object term (weight 20 kept): 128 area-weighted points on the object's own mesh at its true size. The error is the mean of the 3 worst point distances, rewarded as exp(−10e). FDR rescales objects to 5.8 cm; we keep true size, because the score is ADD in metres.
  • Fingertip term in the disabled force_closure slot (weight 6): each fingertip is rewarded exp(−max(0, d_sim − d_ref)/1 cm). It penalises a tip only for being farther from the object than in our reference.
  • Re-weighting: contact wrench support 10 → 3; unintended contact −10 → −2.
  • Not ported: FDR's 10-frame look-ahead encoder, per-hand actor-critic, larger critic and replay, adaptive assist, its terminations and its 400M steps.

4.5 The assist that exploded the spoon (23 Oct)

The first spoon policies scored below doing nothing (eval 3 0.410 against a floor of 0.439; eval 35 0.105 against 0.112), and no object moved in any rollout. A zero-action replay in the training environment found the cause M:

  • FlashCHORD's assist turns the desired angular acceleration into torque with one scalar: the mean of the inertia diagonal.
  • Our spoon mesh with the 0.3 kg placeholder mass has principal inertias of 0.46 / 25.8 / 26.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg·m², a mean-to-minimum ratio of 38. The long axis therefore gets 38× the intended gain, an effective damping of 1,530 s⁻¹.
  • At a 10 ms substep that explicit damping diverges above an assist of about 0.13. So the first four curriculum stages (assist 1.0 → 0.25, about 64M steps) trained on exploding spoons: 1.9 m off by frame 3 on CPU, 10⁸ m by frame 30 on GPU, flinging the right hand 1.5 m and the pot 46 cm.
  • Other objects have ratios of 1.4–2.9 (fine); the dish rack's is 6.4 (marginal).

Fix: torque = R I Rᵀ α with the full inertia tensor (ops/box/fc_v2d_voc.py), training only, on by default. A side effect: training ran 1.9–2× faster (27.5k → 54.7k steps/s on eval 35), because exploding worlds had been eating solver time.

Policies trained before the fix had learned avoidance. Their wrists stayed 26–43 cm from the spoon, since every touch in early training was violent. This pattern returns on eval 10 (Part 7).

4.6 Starts, lead-ins and recovery

  • Lead-in speed (eval 35): the right hand is first seen at frame 6, so the lead-in rushed it in from 18 cm away at 60 cm/s and the simulated wrist fell 16 cm behind. --lead_in_speed 0.2 became a generic default. The retrain only moved 0.127 → 0.147, so the start was not the main limit.
  • Deployment-exact starts (FC_IMMEDIATE_FIRST_FRAME=0.25: a quarter of late resets start exactly like the evaluator). Falsified as a standalone fix on ep18, but it stayed in the lineage of the best ep18 run and so in the standard learner's recipe. The overnight summary and the recipe disagree on whether it was adopted.
  • Recovery training (drop starts that begin with the object off the reference): tested inside grasp-lock runs, ep18 0.220 vs 0.320 and ep12 0.869 vs 0.886. Dropped.

4.7 Where it wins and where it cannot

  • Wins on tools. On the ep39 dust pan the standard learner scored 0.802 ± 0.280, four of five rollouts ≈ 0.94; the early grasp lock scored 0.174. On ep18, 0.776 and 0.889 (two seeds).
  • Cannot hold the spoon. With the assist fixed, the pick-up is learned at 70–90M steps as the assist fades. The drop point then does not move from 90M to 250M (eval 3 frames 105–109, eval 35 frames 141–147). Final scores were 0.29–0.41.
  • Pack 7 episodes: evals 1 (pot + lid), 19 (sweeper + dust pan), 25 (boards, slot carve), 28 (rack + pot) and 30 (sweeper + rack). Packed draws are 0.89–0.93; 10-draw means 0.80–0.93. Evals 1 and 30 are bimodal, with worst draws of 0.15 and 0.26.

4.8 How long to train

  • Of 111 stored checkpoint sweeps, the best checkpoint was the final 250M one in 48 and earlier in 63 M. Easy episodes keep rising to 250M (ep12 0.647 → 0.775 from 100M); hard ones swing.
  • More steps rarely rescue a bad start. Of 22 curves below 0.5 at 100M, the median gain by 250M was +0.04; only 1 ended at or above 0.45 and 13 ended below "do nothing".
  • Early ranking predicts the final. On 31 standard-learner curves, the score at 40 % of training predicts the final with R² 0.79. The leader of a pair at 40 % finished ahead 30 times in 36. Late jumps happened twice (ep18 0.24 → 0.82; eval 25 0.50 → 0.89).
  • Nobody has shown 250M is needed. It is NVIDIA's PPO schedule divided by 10, and our references play at twice NVIDIA's speed. A rescaled 125M schedule was written but never run. Early stopping saves nothing as long as the assist schedule follows the step count.
  • Training metrics were never logged (logging.mode=disabled), so we cannot see why episodes terminate at each stage.
Part 5

Learner 2: the grasp lock

The idea: instead of asking RL to discover a grip, write a designed, physically sound grasp into the reference, so that zero action already carries the object. Then a short RL run only has to finish it. "Secure grasp is more important than replicating it" (the owner, 23 Oct). It now covers 15 of 20 eval episodes, including all ten spoon episodes.

5.1 Birth: the spoon, step by step (23 Oct)

  • Pinch grasps failed. Three pinch versions on eval 3 lifted the spoon 0–1.7 cm. There was no thumb under the handle, and the hand target sat 2 cm below the table top.
  • "Wrap the hand around the handle" (owner). A built power grasp lifted the spoon 24.2 cm against the reference's 27.5 cm. With wrist ILC, zero action reached 0.462, the first result above the floor.
  • Releases. On eval 35, opening all fingers at once flicked the spoon upright (0.218). Relax-then-slide dragged it (0.267). Unrolling the fingertips first fixed the release, but withdrawing still dragged it (0.150). Identical runs differed by 0.150–0.185.
  • Stiff wrist. With kp 400 instead of 25, eval 3's zero action jumped 0.462 → 0.658, and the spoon stayed within 3 cm on 87.5 % of frames instead of 16.9 %.
  • Physics-aware design (note 24). The spoon rolls about its own handle inside the grasp, up to 72°. Minimal-force squeezes designed by linear programming made it worse (0.309, 0.175). The real culprit: fingers closing up through the table rolled the handle about 90° during the pick-up. Closing the knuckles first and the thumb last, with the palm pressed 1 mm into the handle, fixed eval 35 (0.150 → 0.521). The same rule cost eval 3 about 0.2.
  • Copying the human's hand-over failed. Passing the spoon to the left hand's bowl grasp lost it at the switch every time (0.07–0.18), so one hand carries each object from pick-up to release (--single_hand).
  • Training on the designed reference with kp 400: eval 3 reached 0.981 at 70M (0.968 ± 0.020 over 5 draws) and eval 35 0.979 at 40M (0.978 ± 0.002), in about 19 minutes of training. Seed 43 reproduced eval 35 (0.973). On eval 3 it reached only 0.843, because the pot, held by a copied human grasp, slipped.

5.2 The recipe, stage by stage

What ops/jobs/glock_public.sh runs for each episode. Despite the name, the same script runs eval episodes with SPLIT=eval. Only our own reference is read: object path, contact labels and object names.

  1. Build the reference on

    Perception, hands, then the reference builder with wrist cost 3.0 and a capped lead-in, then contact retargeting with rest settling (variant "mck"). Simulator meshes from library_v3.

  2. Support fill on

    Every resting pose (frame 0, the last frame, any still window of ≥ 10 frames) must pass a stability certificate: the centre of mass lies over the support polygon with margin d ≥ 3 mm and tip angle atan(d/h) ≥ 3°, after up to 3° of rocking. Otherwise the smallest certified underside fill of 0.5–2 cm is added to the simulator copy, as long as it stays in free space along the reference. Since 25 Oct an 8-sector "arc foot" can complete an unseen round base.

  3. Find the holds on

    A hand holds an object when it has ≥ 2 contacts on it and the object is carried (> 1.5 cm off its rest, or within 8 frames before or 4 after a move). Gaps ≤ 12 frames are bridged.

  4. Design and lock each grasp on

    • Grasp frame: the frame near the hold start with the most fingertips within 5 mm of the object.
    • Lock: the wrist pose in the object's frame is held from the hold start to its end, so the wrist rides the object's reference path.
    • Squeeze: +0.4 rad on flexion joints.
    • Lift feed-forward: m·g/kp is added to the wrist target while the object is lifted (0.74 cm at kp 400; it was 11.8 cm at kp 25).
    • Release: relax over 6 frames, unroll fingertips before knuckles, withdraw along the palm normal, then blend back to the human motion over 24 frames. An idle hand is parked at its frame-0 pose.

  5. Wrist ILC on

    Four iterations of iterative learning control. Replay at zero action under evaluator conditions, measure where the simulated wrist lags the intended one, and move the wrist targets by 0.8 × the error 2 frames ahead (smoothed over 5 frames, capped at 12 cm / 0.6 rad). A guard keeps the last finite iteration.

  6. Evolutionary grasp search on

    For each hold in turn, CMA-ES searches a wrist offset and rotation in the object's frame plus 22 finger offsets, applied through FlashCHORD's own residual action. Each generation runs 256 worlds: 64 candidates × 4 copies (one exact, three jittered by 5 mm / 0.005 rad), for 20 generations. Fitness is the mean ADD-AUC of all objects against our reference over the hold window; no ground truth. The best edit is baked into the reference before the next hold.

  7. Zero-action gate logged only

    A replay from frame 0 with zero action, scored by sim_vs_ref, plus a rest check (every object stays within 5 mm / 2° until a hand comes for it). In the frozen recipe it is logged, not enforced.

  8. Re-plan in the simulated world off in frozen

    If a simulated object is more than 20 mm / 10° off its reference before its grasp, grasp it where it lies and carry it back over 20 frames. A version is kept only if zero action does not get worse. Used by the "eval_both" variant.

  9. Train on

    FlashSAC from fresh weights, kp 400 embodiment, FDR rewards and assist fix, 70,004,736 steps, seed 42. At most two trainings share a GPU. With the unscaled curriculum the assist is still at 0.1 when training ends, so the policy never trains with it fully off or from exact starts. Arm B's CURR_SCALE=0.4 changed that.

  10. Choose the checkpoint on

    Every 10M checkpoint gets 3 official evaluations and the best mean wins. A single evaluation per checkpoint had been optimistic by about 0.05 (ep21's 30M scored 0.831 once but 0.27 over 5). The winner then gets 5 more evaluations (10 from 30 Oct) and is copied to best/ so store pruning keeps it.

How the wrap is built

Find the handle axis by PCA of the collision hulls, and the radius from the 90th percentile within ±1.5 cm. The grip point is the human's knuckle centre projected onto the axis, kept 4.5 cm from the ends. The hand's y axis runs along the handle, with the palm normal closest to the human's. Each finger and the thumb then curls until a link is within 1 mm of the hull, plus 0.4 rad of squeeze. The approach backs off 4 cm along the palm normal.

5.7 The design reasoning

Why design the grasp instead of learning it

  • Pure RL plateaued on the spoon (160M steps after the pick-up did not move the drop point) or learned avoidance.
  • Training finishes a working reference but does not rescue a broken one: zero action vs trained, Spearman ρ 0.73 over 18 runs.
  • The EA-vs-RL review concluded that evolution suits the small, deceptive, ground-truth-free search for the reference, and RL suits the policy ("Baldwinian split").
  • No learned grasp model supports the Sharpa hand. DemoGrasp ("edit one demonstration") and DexEvolve (evolutionary refinement) were the methods to copy.

Physics and anatomy behind the rules

  • Friction μ 1.0 with pyramidal cones and no torsional friction, so a single opposing pair cannot hold any observed grasp. Torque must come from contacts spread 2–4 cm apart or from form closure.
  • Finger kp 1.7: a uniform squeeze puts 26 N at the fingertip joint against 6.5 N at the knuckle, so fingertips curl off flat objects.
  • The Sharpa thumb cannot oppose the fingertips, only the finger bases: its tip reaches about 15 cm in the hand frame, the fingertips 19–20 cm. Flat objects must go deep into the hand, thumb on top.
  • The successful designed holds have opposition (pot 160°, cup 124°); 7–8 of the 9 failing ones lack the thumb or have under 100°.
  • The 0.889 RL policy on ep18 holds the pan with a key pinch and clamps the sweeper at its neck, not where the human held it. Lesson: copy contact roles and timing, not the human wrist path.

7.2 The eval-10 saga: two cups that RL refuses to stack

  1. Original grasp lock (pack 1): 0.334. The beige cup was an open shell with no base and fell over at frame 2.
  2. Hybrid cup collision copy + standard learner: 0.452 / 0.453 (two seeds). The winner's chosen checkpoint had been pruned from the store, so the loser was packed. The store now keeps checkpoints named by an evaluation file.
  3. Grasp lock on the carved cups (two variants): 0.454 and 0.438. Every checkpoint shows "lift 0": the cup is never lifted. Falsified.
  4. CPU diagnosis. Trained policies never touch the cups. The zero-action plan does carry the beige cup (0.579 / 0.663), but it hits the blue cup's rim with up to 9,650 N of contact force and is flung, because the simulated grip sits 3–6 cm low. Object-level ILC got a replay to 0.632, but 0.42–0.54 under ±3–6 mm jitter. Across our runs, zero action beat the trained policy in 15 of 64 results.
  5. Early checkpoints (every 1M steps, to catch a policy before it drifts from the plan): all 0.446–0.465. Falsified.
  6. Zero-initialised residual head (27 Oct: the policy starts at exactly the plan, with exploration 0.05): 0.436–0.455 from 1M to 30M; chosen 0.4459. Falsified.

Diagnosis: whatever the start, RL on eval 10 moves away from the plan within about 1M steps and settles on not touching the cups. The rim collision makes every contact attempt expensive (terminations, unintended-contact penalties), so avoidance is the reward-maximising answer. We don't submit the zero-action plan itself (0.663), because the kit asks for a trained policy. The next attempt needs a task-side change: relaxed termination in the nesting phase, or more clearance. Gate: the public twin ep11 must gain ≥ +0.05 on 2 seeds first. Solving it would be worth about +0.017 on the leaderboard E.

Part 8

Variance: the hidden variable

The official evaluator is not reproducible: the same checkpoint gives different rollouts on different runs. Kaggle scores the one rollout we submit, as it is. So a policy's mean and its packed draw are different things, and we had been comparing policies on 5 draws and packing 1.

  • Our board score sits on lucky draws. Ranked within their 10 fresh draws, the packed draws sit far higher than chance (rank sums 69 of 80 against an expected 40, z ≈ 3.2). The luck is concentrated in the high-variance episodes 1, 14, 25, 30 and 33. If the organisers re-run our checkpoints, expect about 0.78 rather than 0.81 E.
  • The training machine is not the official evaluator. On the training box the packed eval-1 and eval-30 policies showed 0 bad draws in 10. The official evaluator gave them 5 bad draws in 22. So every training-box confirmation now needs an official re-check, which is what tonight's eval-1 test is.
  • Single evaluations are optimistic. Picking a checkpoint by one draw overstated it by about 0.026–0.05. That is why grasp-lock checkpoints get 3 draws and finalists 5–10.
  • Packing rules that follow from this:
    • pack one fresh official rollout per changed checkpoint, never the best of several ("we cannot pick and choose");
    • an unchanged checkpoint keeps its earlier rollout, matched by checksum;
    • a replacement counts as a board gain only if its multi-draw mean beats the draw it replaces.
  • What it means for the learners: more seeds and more draws cannot raise the board much, because the luck is already banked. Gains must come from policies whose draws agree, or from the reference side. A lower-variance learner is worth more than a higher-mean one if the organisers re-run checkpoints.
Part 9

What we learned about learning here

Fix the plan before training it

Most early failures were in the reference or the geometry, not the learner: the floor fit, overlapping hands, penetrating fingers, objects that cannot stand, floating objects, missing slots, swapped boards. A zero-action replay finds these in minutes for cents.

RL learns avoidance when contact is punished

Exploding spoons and eval 10's rim collisions both taught policies to keep their hands away. Avoidance appears within about 1M steps and does not unlearn with more steps, a different start or a zero-initialised head.

A good plan predicts a good policy

Training finishes a working reference; it rarely rescues a broken one (ρ 0.73). ep18 is the exception: holds starting at 0.50–0.58 were finished. More steps rarely rescue a bad start either.

Stiffness is task-dependent

kp 400 is the difference between 0.4 and 0.98 on the spoon, yet it hurt plain RL on ep18 and ep24 and zero action on eval 35. One wrist for every task is the wrong question.

One draw is not a measurement

Draws of one checkpoint range up to 0.15–0.92 (eval 1). Seeds of one recipe range 0.40–0.80 (eval 32). Compare on means of ≥ 5 draws, confirm finalists on 10–20 draws, and check on the official evaluator.

Twins can mislead

The lathe pitcher improved its public twin from 0.776 to 0.874 and cost 0.0045 on the board. Changes now need two twins or a reason that doesn't depend on the reference.

Ground-truth-free selection works within its reach

sim_vs_ref ranked 63 of 63 public policy pairs correctly (r 0.997). But it is blind to reference error, which we estimate at about 0.10 of the board, as large as all policy loss combined.

Hand-applied fixes don't generalise by themselves

Re-detection, track guard, identity rule, arc foot and carves were switched on where forensics looked. A held-out evaluation after the close runs only defaults, so each fix needs an automatic trigger.
