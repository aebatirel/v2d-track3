V2D Track 3 Briefing

We turn a head-camera video of a person handling objects into a trained policy for two simulated robot hands. The official evaluator rolls the policy out, and Kaggle scores the simulated object paths against motion capture. Our best leaderboard AUC is 0.8146 (7th), up from 0.575 on our first upload. This page covers how we got there, what it cost, what failed, and where we need ideas.

The task

What is simulated and how it is scored

Each episode is a short egocentric clip (a stereo pair of mono cameras plus one colour camera) of a person doing a kitchen task: lifting a pot lid, stirring with a wooden spoon, stacking cups, sweeping, slotting two boards together. There are 20 public episodes with mocap ground truth and 20 hidden eval episodes that decide the leaderboard.

  • Simulator: NVIDIA FlashCHORD at the evaluator pin 8c778f9 (Newton 1.2 on MuJoCo Warp 3.8.1, JAX 0.10.2). Two floating Sharpa hands with 22 finger joints each, the objects as rigid bodies with a 0.3 kg placeholder mass and CoACD convex collision hulls, a support table and gravity. Physics runs at 100 Hz and the policy at 20 Hz.
  • Training: one policy per episode. FlashSAC (off-policy actor-critic, actor 256 / critic 512 hidden) in 4,096 parallel worlds. It learns a residual on top of a reference motion: wrist up to ±20 cm / ±1 rad from the reference, plus finger joints. A virtual object controller (VOC) pushes objects along the reference early in training and is annealed away by a curriculum. On an RTX PRO 6000 this runs about 45k steps/s; a 250M-step policy takes 1.5–2 h alone or about 3.3 h with two per GPU M.
  • Evaluation: the official evaluator runs 1 world with no assist, so the hands must really grip. Rows must be official-evaluator rollouts of a trained policy, with checkpoint and code digests and a GitHub commit URL. Perception output alone cannot be submitted.
  • Metric: the scorer registers our submitted mesh to the hidden scan (ICP), aligns the scene once at frame 0 on the first non-symmetric object, then computes ADD-AUC swept over 1–9 cm. Five pose boards (AUC, SP-SR, MP-SR, RPE, MPPE) plus a mesh board (CD-O). The FAQ says the final rank is the lowest sum of the six board ranks. Leaving every object where it starts scores about 0.446.
Our pipeline

From video to a submitted rollout

  1. PerceptionFoundationStereo depth; frame-0 detection by SAM2 proposals × DINOv2 against renders of our meshes; FoundationPose tracking; visual odometry for the head.
  2. MeshesOur own reconstructions (TSDF fusion along our tracks, lathe / part fits), because the FAQ requires reconstructed meshes. Simulator-only collision "carves" for slots and nesting.
  3. HandsMediaPipe 0.10.14 on the colour camera, lifted to 3-D with stereo depth; NVIDIA's own retargeter to the Sharpa hands.
  4. Referencemkref: table frame, contact-anchored retargeting (fingertips snapped to surfaces, no penetration), optional designed grasp ("grasp lock").
  5. LearnersTwo recipes per episode (below), trained on rented RTX PRO 6000s.
  6. Choose and packPick per episode by the mean of several official evaluations against our own reference (no ground truth), then the kit packer and a Kaggle upload.

The selection yardstick, sim_vs_ref, is the kit's AUC of the simulated rollout against our reference. It sees robot error but cannot see errors in the reference itself. On public twins, true AUC ≈ 0.979 × sim_vs_ref when the reference is sound M.

The beginning

Why the first score was 0.575

The first upload scored well below our own projection of ≈ 0.68. On public episodes perception lost only 3–4 %; on eval it lost about 19 %. Forensics on the 20 eval videos and rollouts found four groups of causes, all eval-specific:

  • Broken meshes. The pitcher was a twisted shell, the beige cup an open shell that fell over at rest (6 of 6 times), and the boards had no slots (evals 20, 10, 25).
  • Perception. Objects were tracked onto the person's left wrist or white watch (eval 30 from frame 0; 33 and 14 when the cup was hidden). Eval 25's two boards were swapped and one turned 180°, so that episode scored near zero against the truth.
  • Grasps. The grasp-lock recipe never lifted the lid (1), the dust pan (19) or the boards, and the spoon in 33 failed.
  • The spoon. It sits in 10 of 20 eval episodes (32.5 % of the score) and has no public mocap. Its accuracy can only be inferred, at about 0.73–0.81 E.

Before any eval run, the public end-to-end tests had their own early failures: the very first ep12 score was AUC 0.009 because the reference builder fitted the floor instead of the table (objects fell 60 cm), and on 23 Oct the spoon exploded in training because FlashCHORD's VOC scales torque by the mean inertia while the spoon's long axis has 1/38 of it. Both are fixed.

  • Policy loss, 0.083 M. The sum of (1 − sim_vs_ref)/20. Eval 10 alone is 0.027; then 32, 25, 20, 16, 1, 14 and 30. Raising eval 10 to 0.90 would add about +0.017, enough to pass 4th place of three days ago but not 5th today.
  • Reference loss, ≈ 0.10 E. Perception, frame-0 anchor and mesh errors that our yardstick cannot see. The model puts ≈ 0.06–0.07 of it on the spoon (10 episodes), ≈ 0.009 on the pitcher and ≈ 0.008 on the boards. This is solved for, not measured: there is no spoon mocap, and the implied spoon accuracy drifted from 0.81 to 0.80 between packs with no spoon change. That drift is the model's error bar.
  • Our board score sits on lucky draws M. The official evaluator is not reproducible between runs. Ten fresh draws per packed checkpoint give a mean of 0.8755 against the packed 0.9164. If the organisers re-run checkpoints at validation, expect ≈ 0.78, not 0.81 E. Eval 1, 14 and 30 have sd 0.20–0.26.
  • Small swaps cannot close the gap. Kaggle scores the submitted trajectories as they are, so a better policy only helps if its fresh draw beats the draw already packed. Recent swaps were worth +0.0003 to +0.0007 each.
  • CD-O is our worst board (11th, 0.402 cm). With the final rank being a sum of six board ranks, it costs us more than the AUC board suggests. Leaders are at 0.11 cm.
  • Fixes were applied by hand. The re-detection, track guard, identity rule, arc foot and carves were each switched on for the episodes where forensics saw the problem. The sponsor runs a held-out evaluation after the close on undistributed data, where only defaults run.
  • Open rule questions. The kp 400 wrist embodiment used by 15 of our 20 policies, mesh provenance (library_v3 was built along tracks seeded by scan templates) and the parametric spoon template. A question list for the organisers is drafted; no answer is recorded.
Experiments

What worked and what did not

Every idea was tested on public episodes against mocap first, with a falsification rule written down before the run. Numbers are AUC against mocap on public episodes unless marked sim_vs_ref.

Adopted

  • Compactness prior for frame-0 detection: public perception 0.883 → 0.932 (ep26 0.065 → 0.988).
  • Table-plane fix in the reference builder: ep12 0.009 → 0.890.
  • Contact-anchored retargeting: hand penetration p95 20 → 6 mm on ep24.
  • FlashDexRetarget-style rewards: ep12 + ep18, mean of four runs 0.753 → 0.830.
  • VOC inertia fix: the spoon no longer explodes in training.
  • Grasp lock + wrist ILC + kp 400 wrist + fine-tune: spoon evals 0.97–0.98 sim_vs_ref; the kp 25 wrist fails the same recipe (0.23–0.42).
  • Standard learner kept for tools and boards: ep39 0.17 → 0.80.
  • Track guard (below-table poses re-registered): 8 episodes 0.724 → 0.782.
  • On-table detection rule: ep11 0.102 → 0.898.
  • Board identity rule, arc foot (ep21 twin 0.657 → 0.772), new beige cup, slot carve (board 104° → 0.5° off on CPU).
  • Timing advance of 0.545 frames (S2): +0.006 on 20 public episodes, 17 of 20 better. Not yet in eval references.
  • Multi-draw confirmation before swapping a policy (H5).

Falsified or no gain

  • Viterbi hand-identity linker: fixed one hand-over, broke others.
  • Wrist IK cost 3.0 as default: one catastrophic seed on ep12.
  • Mid-stiffness wrist on hard public episodes: ep18 0.19, ep24 0.05.
  • Rest settling, recovery training from dropped starts, flattened board meshes.
  • Event-anchored grasp windows: 0 of 4 twins improved.
  • Eval 10: grasp lock on carved cups, early checkpoints, zero-residual start, interruptible seed race. None beat 0.456.
  • Lathe pitcher mesh: −0.0045 on the board despite a better twin.
  • Refine-stage snap and tilt: −0.0077 and −0.0013.
  • Eval-14 carved retrains: 0.58–0.62, below the 0.645 bar.
  • Recipe arms (shorter curriculum end, lower ball-wrist damping): inconclusive.
  • Image-to-3D generators (SAM 3D, Pixal3D-MV, TRELLIS.2, Hunyuan3D-2.1, TripoSG): mean CD-O 0.49–0.98 cm vs our library's 0.477; pose error 10–29 mm vs ≈ 6 mm.
  • Grasp-type classifier on our hands (too noisy); minimal-force squeezes; VLM task phases (+0.0002).
  • Longer training: more steps rarely rescue a bad start, and stopping at 100M is not safe either.

For the expert

Where outside ideas would help most

  1. Getting RL to keep a plan it is afraid of.Eval 10: our open-loop plan with zero action carries the beige cup into the blue cup and scores 0.663, but the kit requires a trained policy, and every learner learns to avoid the stack within about 1M steps (the cups collide at the rim with 6–7 mm misalignment and get flung). Residual-around-plan, BC or KL regularisation toward the plan, critic burn-in and termination changes are candidates. Which would you try first?
  2. Robustness to a non-deterministic evaluator.The same checkpoint scores 0.26–0.92 across official draws on some episodes (sd up to 0.255). Our training box gave 0/10 bad draws where the official evaluator gave 5/22. How would you train or select for worst-case rather than mean behaviour?
  3. Measuring a thin wooden spoon without ground truth.Ten episodes, 32.5 % of the score, often hidden inside a pot or cup, seen by a stereo pair of mono cameras plus one colour camera. Our inferred accuracy is 0.73–0.81. What GT-free proxy would you trust to say the spoon pose is wrong?
  4. Meshes that both register and simulate.The scorer registers our mesh to the hidden scan, so a mesh error costs pose score too. Thin, symmetric and hollow objects (boards with slots, pitcher, cups) are the hard cases; image-to-3D generators lost to our TSDF and part-built meshes.
  5. Two-handed holds and placements.Evals 32 and 33 hold a cup in one hand and a spoon in the other for about 270 frames; evals 15 and 16 lose the spoon on release into a holder. Reward or grasp-design ideas that improve release stability?
  6. Wrist stiffness and the rules.Stock wrist kp 25 N/m saturates its ±20 cm action range on 23 of 27 moving public tracks; our grasp-lock recipe uses kp 400. Comparable systems run 300–350 N/m. Is a custom embodiment acceptable to a judge, and is there a better middle ground?
